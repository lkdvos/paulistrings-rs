## Microbenchmarks (`cargo bench -p paulistrings`)

| bench | n | time | throughput |
|---|---|---|---|
| `pauli_string_mul_assign` W=1 | — | 9.03 ns | 110.7 Melem/s |
| `pauli_string_mul_assign` W=2 | — | 14.89 ns | 67.2 Melem/s |
| `pauli_sum_add` | 10⁴ | 60.9 µs | 328.3 Melem/s |
| `pauli_sum_add` | 10⁶ | 19.98 ms | 100.1 Melem/s |
| `apply_layer_clifford1q_h` | 10⁴ | 807.8 µs | 12.4 Melem/s |
| `apply_layer_clifford1q_h` | 10⁶ | 63.24 ms | 15.8 Melem/s |

### The headline number

`apply_layer` under `Clifford1Q::h` at 10⁶ terms runs at **15.8 Melem/s**, while
`pauli_sum_add` — a full two-pointer merge over a comparable payload, *including*
three `with_capacity` allocations — runs at **100.1 Melem/s** on the same host.

That is a **6.3× per-element gap**, and it is paid for nothing:

- `Clifford1Q::h` has `max_fanout = 1`, so there is no fanout to absorb.
- Clifford conjugation is a **bijection** on Pauli keys, so no two outputs can
  collide — the merge phase is provably a no-op, and the test suite confirms it
  (`out.len() == 1` in `tests/propagate.rs:47`).
- `apply` itself is a single 4-entry table lookup plus two masked bit writes
  (`clifford.rs:110`), which is cheaper per term than the merge's comparison.

So essentially the entire difference is `sort_phase` (`sort_merge.rs:215`) — a
sequential, stable, indirect-comparator sort plus a second full materialization
of the payload — and the three zero-filled `n × max_fanout` scratch buffers it
sorts. This is the quantity v0.2 is designed to remove.

Note also that `mul_assign` at 9 ns (W=1) is ~32 cycles at 3.6 GHz for four
popcounts and two XORs per word. It is on the rotation and general-unitary path
today; v0.2 removes it from the hot loop entirely by folding `i^k` into the
prepared amplitude table (v0.2 §2.6).

### What the v0.1 bench surface did *not* cover

The single layer bench was `Clifford1Q::h`, which is simultaneously the worst
case for the sort (a permutation of an almost-sorted array) and the best case for
the merge (every run has length 1). One point. It also passed the channel
*concretely*, so the call devirtualized — whereas `propagate` erases to
`&dyn Channel<W>` (`engine/mod.rs:78`) and pays a vtable call **per input term**.
Slice A.2 widened this; see below.

## Macro benchmark: `examples/ising_2d_quench.rs`

`cargo run --release --example ising_2d_quench`, 40 first-order Trotter steps,
`And(CoefficientThreshold(1e-10), TopN(n))`:

| lattice | channels/step | layers total | peak terms | wall clock | per layer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4×4 | 48 | 1 920 | 50 000 (TopN) | **21.2 s** | ~11 ms |
| 6×6 | 108 | 4 320 | 200 000 (TopN) | **136.1 s** | ~31.5 ms |

Both saturate their `TopN` cap within a few steps and stay there, so this
measures steady-state layer cost at a fixed `n`, multiplied by a large layer
count — the regime real workloads live in, and the one where v0.1's ~11 + 3k
per-layer allocations and two `O(n log n)` sorts per layer (the engine's, plus
`TopN::finalize_layer`'s) compound.

**The run reproduces the committed `examples/output/ising_{4x4,6x6}.csv`
byte-for-byte** (`git diff` on that directory is empty afterwards). That makes it
a hard end-to-end correctness gate for the rewrite, not just a timing harness —
4320 layers of real propagation with truncation active is the strongest
whole-system signal in the repo.
